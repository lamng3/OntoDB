\section{Introduction}
\label{sec-intro}

Ontologies such as the Gene Ontology~\cite{ashburner2000} and the schemas of
enterprise knowledge graphs are stored as sets of triples and queried in
three ways.
A \emph{pattern} query asks for the triples that match a template, such as
the resources of one type, and is the base case of every RDF
store~\cite{weiss2008,neumann2010}.
A \emph{structural} query asks for the instances at or below a class, and
needs the class hierarchy.
A \emph{semantic} query asks for the terms closest in meaning to a given
term, which has no answer in the data and needs a similarity measure over
embeddings.
The three meet in queries such as ``the terms most similar to
\emph{apoptotic process} that lie under \emph{cell death}''.

Existing systems leave four problems open.
\textbf{C1. Hierarchy costs space or time.}
RDFS entailment is either \emph{materialized} at write time, which stores
every inferred triple~\cite{rdfox}, or \emph{rewritten} into the query,
which scans once per subclass.
On the Gene Ontology with 200,000 typed entities, materializing the closure
multiplies the triples by 8.1, and rewriting reads 48 times more pages than
a store that knows the hierarchy.
\textbf{C2. A sorted index does not hold meaning.}
Triple stores answer patterns with range scans over sorted permutations.
It is tempting to believe that neighbors in meaning are neighbors in the
order; we prove they cannot all be (Section~\ref{sec-order}), and measure how
few are.
Similarity is therefore left to a vector database next to the triple store,
with its own storage, its own paging, and no view of the hierarchy.
\textbf{C3. A class filter is correlated with the vectors.}
Filtered vector search is usually costed by assuming that the filter is
independent of the similarity.
In an ontology it is not: the neighbors of a term lie under the term's own
ancestors.
A post-filtered index can then find 7\% of the right answers, and the
independence estimate of the work it must do is wrong in both directions
(Section~\ref{sec-vectors}).
\textbf{C4. A paged store must scale across threads.}
A classic buffer pool serializes every page access on one latch, and a
classic B+ tree write latches the root.
In our first version, lookups per second fell from 330 thousand with one
thread to 180 thousand with eight, and inserts from 184 thousand to 23
thousand: a store embedded in a service or a notebook that answers many
queries at once spent its time waiting for itself.

\begin{example}
\label{exa-motivation}
Let $q$ be the term \emph{apoptotic process} in the Gene Ontology and $c$ the
class \emph{cell death}.
The query asks for the five terms nearest to $q$ in embedding space among
the 97 classes at or below $c$ in an 834-class slice of the ontology.
A triple store alone cannot rank by meaning.
A vector index alone returns neighbors from anywhere in the ontology, and
a post-filter keeps those under $c$ only if enough of them are.
\sys finds the 97 classes with one range scan of the same B+ tree that holds
the triples, scores them exactly, and returns \emph{apoptotic process
involved in development} (cosine 0.865) first, in under a millisecond in a
web page.
\end{example}

Database systems have handled a similar split for decades: an access method
is added to the engine that owns the storage, paging, and logging, and not
beside it.
This paper takes that view of ontologies.

\stitle{Contributions}.
This paper introduces \sys, an open-source ontology database in C++20
(Figure~\ref{fig-arch}), and makes the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A B+ tree triple store with its algorithms}
  (Section~\ref{sec-store}).
  Three permutations of dictionary-encoded triples sit behind a buffer pool
  with LRU-K eviction.
  We give the algorithms for search, insert, delete, range scan, and bulk
  load, the invariants they keep, and a bound on the pages a range scan
  reads.
\item \textbf{A concurrency design that scales}
  (Section~\ref{sec-conc}).
  An optimistic descent latches only the leaf; a buffer pool of shards
  serves resident pages with an atomic pin and no latch; a one-word
  reader-writer latch replaces a mutex-based one; the dictionary decodes
  under striped locks.
  With eight threads \sys serves 1.46 million lookups per second, 3.5 times
  its single-thread rate and 3 to 7 times the rate of Oxigraph, Jena TDB2,
  and SQLite, and its insert rate grows 3.6 times.
  The change passes the engine's tests under ThreadSanitizer and
  AddressSanitizer.
\item \textbf{Hierarchies as intervals} (Section~\ref{sec-labels}).
  A postorder labelling of the class DAG gives each class a set of
  intervals, kept in the same B+ tree, so that ``everything under $c$'' is
  a few range scans.
  We bound the number of intervals by the number of non-tree edges and
  store no entailed triple: 8.1 times fewer triples than materialization,
  48 times fewer page reads than rewriting, and 2 to 48 times faster than
  other engines' subclass-path queries.
\item \textbf{Vector indexes in the engine, and how to filter them}
  (Sections~\ref{sec-order} and~\ref{sec-vectors}).
  We prove that no linear order keeps neighbors close, add exact, IVF,
  product-quantized, and HNSW search, store IVF posting lists as key ranges
  of the triple B+ tree, show that filtering by a class is correlated with
  similarity, and give the rule that picks between pre-filtering and masked
  search; exact pre-filtering is $10^2$ to $10^4$ times faster than any
  index strategy below about 1,000 members.
\item \textbf{A build for the browser} (Section~\ref{sec-system}).
  The engine, with its paged B+ trees and its semantic extension, compiles
  to a 0.85\,MB WebAssembly module.
\item \textbf{Strong performance} (Section~\ref{sec-exp}).
  Against Oxigraph~\cite{oxigraph}, Apache Jena TDB2~\cite{jenatdb}, and
  SQLite on LUBM~\cite{lubm} and the Gene Ontology, \sys answers selective
  triple patterns 3 to 37 times faster, instance queries 2 to 48 times
  faster, and sustains 3 to 7 times the lookup throughput under eight
  threads, with the smallest files of the disk-based stores
  (Table~\ref{tab-glance}).
\end{enumerate}

\stitle{Novelty}.
Interval labelling of a hierarchy~\cite{agrawal1989}, the B+ tree, latch
crabbing~\cite{lehman1981}, and IVF and HNSW indexes~\cite{jegou2011,malkov2020}
are known.
What is new is their combination in one engine and what that makes
possible: the class hierarchy as range scans of the tree that holds the
triples, so that a class filter is a binary search and not a scan or a
stored closure; IVF posting lists that page, cache, latch, and update like
triples; a measured account of why a class filter breaks the independence
assumption of filtered vector search; a buffer pool whose resident-page hit
is one compare-and-swap, which no other open ontology store we ran has; and
the same engine in a browser.
In the documentation we read, none of the systems we compare with offers
more than two of these.
The code, data generators, and benchmark programs are available at the
artifact URL above.
